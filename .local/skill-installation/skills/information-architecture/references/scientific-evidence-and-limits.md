The sources below are real scholarly publications with DOI/publisher confirmation checked on 2026-10-31. They inform *how to formulate and test hypotheses* about information architecture; they do **not** establish universal design heuristics or measurable performance gains for an untested application.

## 1. Card sorting as a diagnostic and evaluation method

**Wentzel, M. J., Müller, F., Beerlage-de Jong, N., & van Gemert-Pijnen, J. (2016).** "Card sorting to evaluate the robustness of the information architecture of a protocol website." *International Journal of Medical Informatics*, **86**, 71–81. DOI: [10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2015.12.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2015.12.003), [PubMed 26725698](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26725698/).

An evaluation of a German/Dutch public/health-worker protocol site using 48 card-sort participants with search-log-derived cards. Categories and terminology were examined after use; some additional categories emerged. **Supported:** card sorting can elicit grouping and vocabulary and detect changes in needs. **Not supported:** a proven improvement in task-completion rate or every use case/role from a card sort alone.

## 2. Negative evidence about card-sort congruence

**Schmettow, M., & Sommer, J. (2016).** "Linking card sorting to browsing performance – are congruent municipal websites more efficient to use?" *Behaviour & Information Technology*, **35**(6), 452–470. DOI: [10.1080/0144929X.2016.1157207](https://doi.org/10.1080/0144929X.2016.1157207).

Card sorting (n=27) was compared with navigation performance in municipal sites (n=50). The authors did **not** observe a relationship between congruence with elicited mental models and browsing performance in that study. **Supported:** do not equate card-sort resemblance with usability success; evaluate labels, complexity and real navigation tasks. **Not supported:** card sorting is always useless, or all menu taxonomies are equivalent.

## 3. Information architecture and observed task performance

**Toms, E. G. (1999).** "The impact of information architecture on academic web site usability." *The Electronic Library*, **17**(5), 293–304. DOI: [10.1108/02640479910330714](https://doi.org/10.1108/02640479910330714).

Twenty-four participants at a specific academic site attempted typical information tasks; issues were found in grouping, labels and access aids. **Supported:** evaluate whether labels/navigation help users accomplish concrete tasks. **Not supported:** extrapolating the study's task success rate to contemporary sites, technical CI systems or other participant populations.

## 4. Theoretical basis for information scent

**Pirolli, P., & Card, S. (1999).** "Information foraging." *Psychological Review*, **106**(4), 643–675. DOI: [10.1037/0033-295X.106.4.643](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.106.4.643).

A theoretical/cognitive model of information gathering and proximal cues. **Supported:** information scent as a useful theoretical framing for navigation hypotheses. **Not supported:** any specific numeric layout optimum, a seven-item cap, a guaranteed optimal depth, or an independently validated success metric for this skill's fixture.

## Practical synthesis, including conflicting findings

Design information architecture from observed tasks and vocabulary; use card sorting for exploratory categories and labels; test tree/prototype navigation to verify actual outcomes, with permission and scope contexts. Treat “findability” as an empirical outcome with population/task/environment limits. Support conflicting results transparently; do not select only studies consistent with a favored design approach.

**Not executed:** no new participant experiment, cognitive model fitting or statistical meta-analysis was conducted while preparing this skill. Bibliographic verification is not experimental replication.

**Explicit limitations:** Study contexts, sampling strategies, tasks, website eras and measures vary. Bibliographic verification cannot establish causal effects in the Ec or in a new application.
